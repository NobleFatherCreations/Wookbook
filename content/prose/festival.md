### BUYING AND SELLING AT YOUR FIRST FEST

### YOUR ART ON OUR MERCH

**Archetype:** Image and IP Theft / Unauthorized Reproduction

**Clinical name:** Intellectual Property Theft / Copyright Violation

Your designs, your process images, your finished work appearing on someone else's merchandise, social media, or promotional materials — without your permission, without credit, and often generating revenue for them while you receive nothing.

You find your work on a festival's merchandise table — a tote bag and two t-shirts featuring a painting you created for them two years ago. You were paid for the original painting. Payment for an original artwork does not transfer reproduction rights unless explicitly stated in writing. Copyright in the original work belongs to you. The buyer purchased the physical object — not the right to reproduce it commercially. This is not a gray area. It is copyright law. The protection is a written agreement before any commission that specifies exactly what rights the buyer receives and what they do not.

**Tells:** Your work appearing on merchandise, promotional materials, or commercial contexts without notice / "We paid for the painting" used to justify unlimited reproduction rights / Your work appearing in advertising, sponsorship materials, or branded content / Digital versions of your work being sold, licensed, or reproduced without your knowledge

**Check:** C.R.E.A.T.E. — "A" CHECK: "Has anyone asked me in writing for permission to reproduce this work — or did they just...

**The dark version (Reproduction Rights — What To Include In Every Commission):** Your commission agreement should specify: (1) what rights the buyer receives — typically "right to display the original artwork" only, (2) that all reproduction rights remain with the artist, (3) any commercial use (merchandise, advertising, print) requires a separate license with additional fee, (4) your name must appear with any digital reproduction. US Copyright registration ($65 at copyright.gov) gives you the ability to sue for statutory damages ($750-$30,000 per infringement) — making enforcement financially viable.

**The move:** Define reproduction rights in writing before any commission. Payment for the original piece is not payment for reproduction rights.

**Say:** Reproduction rights are separate from the original — if you'd like to use this work on merchandise we'll need a separate licensing agreement.

**The truth:** You created it. You own the right to reproduce it. Every commercial use requires your permission and your compensation.

### THE REAL COLLAB

### YOUR REPUTATION OR YOUR COMPLIANCE

**Clinical name:** Coercion / Reputation Destruction

### THE BAND MEMBER WHO TAKES

**Section:** CONTROL

**Archetype:** Internal Financial Exploitation / Band Dynamics

**Clinical name:** Financial Exploitation / Internal Power Abuse

The financial and power dynamics within a band or creative partnership are one of the most commonly neglected protection areas for musicians. Exploitation by a bandmate or creative partner is just as real as exploitation by a promoter — and harder to name because the relationship is closer.

You write most of the songs. You book most of the shows. You handle most of the promotion. The split has always been equal. You never thought to question it — you're a band, you're family. Then a manager offers you a solo project and you realize that without you the band doesn't function, and with the band you're sharing everything you generate equally with people who generate a fraction of what you do.

Band agreements are one of the most neglected legal documents in independent music. Who owns the name? Who owns the masters? What's the songwriting split? What happens when someone leaves? Who controls social media? Without answers in writing, whoever controls the logistics controls the band — and the person who does the most work often has the least formal protection. This isn't just about money. It's about who owns your creative legacy.

**Tells:** Contributions to the band's output and income are significantly unequal but compensation isn't / One member controls band finances, social media, or industry relationships / Leaving the band feels like losing ownership of work you created / Creative credit doesn't reflect actual creative contribution

**Check:** S.O.U.N.D. — "O" CHECK: "Do I know what I actually own in this creative partnership — in writing?"

**The dark version (Band Agreement Basics):** A band agreement should address: (1) who owns the band name and what happens to it if someone leaves. (2) Songwriting and publishing splits — and whether they're per-song or blanket. (3) Who owns the master recordings. (4) Revenue split for touring vs recording vs publishing vs merch. (5) Decision-making process — majority vote, unanimous, or designated authority. (6) Exit terms — what a departing member takes with them. Volunteer Lawyers for the Arts has band agreement templates. A music attorney review costs a fraction of what disputes cost later.

**The move:** Get your band agreement in writing before you have a dispute about what you each own.

**Say:** I think we should get our band agreement written down so we're all protected — I can get a template and we can go through it together.

**The truth:** The band feels like family until there's money involved. The paperwork is how you protect the family.

### YOUR STANDARD RIDER

**Section:** CONTROL

**Archetype:** What to Include, How to Use It

**Clinical name:** Professional Sovereignty / Contract Protection

Your rider is not just technical requirements. It is the document that defines your professional terms, protects your rights, and sets the expectations for every engagement before you arrive. Most musicians don't use it fully. This page changes that.

You've been showing up to gigs and figuring things out on arrival — the recording situation, the merch arrangement, the accommodation, the payment timing. Each thing individually seems manageable to negotiate in person. Collectively, negotiating all of these while you're also trying to prepare for a performance means you're always behind and always slightly at a disadvantage. Your rider is supposed to solve this before you arrive.

A comprehensive rider serves two functions: it communicates your professional requirements AND it creates a paper trail of what was agreed. When things don't match the rider at the venue, you have documentation of what was promised. When recording happens without your agreement, you have documentation of what your rights are. The rider is not a diva document — it's a professional standard that protects both parties.

**Tells:** Technical requirements: sound system specs, monitoring, load-in time, soundcheck time / Financial terms: fee, payment method and timing, deposit requirements and deadline / Recording and broadcast: explicit statement that Artist retains all recording rights / Accommodation and hospitality: what's provided, what's not, quality standards

**Check:** O.U.N.D. — "S" CHECK: "Does my rider include recording rights language, payment terms, and all my technical...

**The dark version (What A Complete Rider Covers):** Technical rider: stage plot, input list, monitoring requirements, backline requirements, load-in/soundcheck/show times. Business rider: fee and payment terms, deposit amount and deadline, cancellation policy, accommodation and transportation specs, press photo and bio requirements. Rights rider: recording rights clause (you retain all), broadcast rights, how your name can be used in promotion. Hospitality rider: green room requirements, food and beverage, guest list. Template riders are available from AFM (afm.org) and from music attorney blogs. Customize and use yours consistently.

**The move:** Send your rider with every booking confirmation. Update it annually. Use it as your baseline.

**Say:** I'll send my rider along with the signed contract — let me know if anything needs discussion before the show.

**The truth:** The rider is not arrogance. It is professionalism. It protects you and it protects the promoter.

### BUYING AND SELLING SAFELY ON THE CIRCUIT

**Section:** TOOLS

**Archetype:** Legal Risk, Undercovers, and Festival Transactions

**Clinical name:** Legal Risk Awareness / Harm Reduction

Musicians and touring artists face specific legal risks at festivals around buying, selling, and the substance culture of the touring life. This page is practical harm reduction for people who live on the circuit.

After the show, in the green room, someone offers you something. You're still running on the set, the green room feels safe and private, and the person seems completely trustworthy — they've been on tour with you for weeks. The substances have been tested this run, you know what you're getting. Two hours later you're in a situation that's more complicated than the green room felt.

Artists on tour are subject to the same drug laws as everyone else regardless of backstage location. Green room status does not create legal protection. The touring lifestyle creates normalization of substance use that can gradually erode your assessment of what's risky. Know what you're taking (test it), Know your legal exposure, and be aware that the people around you on tour have their own legal exposure to consider — which may or may not align with your interests.

**Tells:** Green room transactions feel safer because they're "backstage" — they're not legally safer / Someone you trust on tour can still put you in a position of legal exposure / Substances offered in the context of a close working relationship still carry risk / The touring lifestyle normalizes substance use in ways that can gradually erode risk-assessment

**Check:** S.O.U.N.D. — "U" CHECK: "Do I understand who is actually in this space and what the legal implications of this...

**The dark version (If You're Detained At A Festival Or Venue):** Know this before you need it: (1) You have the right to remain silent — use it. "I'd like to speak with a lawyer." (2) Your manager or label does not automatically have the right to make legal decisions for you — that's your call. (3) Your tour rider and performance contract are your documents — you have the right to a copy of any contract you've signed. (4) Know the National Lawyers Guild (nlg.org) — they have observer programs at many major events. (5) Good Samaritan laws protect people seeking overdose help in most states — always call for medical help in an overdose situation.

**The move:** Know your legal exposure before any situation requires you to think about it.

**Say:** I'd like to speak with a lawyer before I answer any questions.

**The truth:** The backstage is not a legal gray zone. Know your rights.

### THE STAGE IS NOT A CONSENT BYPASS

The music industry has a long and documented history of artists using stage power and access to override or bypass consent. This is not ancient history — it is ongoing. You don't have to be a predator to contribute to this dynamic. The post-show adrenaline state impairs your judgment. The power you hold in that space is real even when you're not wielding it deliberately. The ask is simple: slow down, check in with words, receive the answer, and respect it completely.

### THE MENTAL HEALTH REALITY OF TOURING

**Section:** ACCOUNTABILITY

**Archetype:** What Nobody Talks About and What Actually Helps

Touring is one of the most mentally demanding sustained experiences a person can undertake. The highs are extraordinary. The lows are real and they're often invisible — because the expectation is that you're living the dream and therefore shouldn't be struggling.

You're three weeks into a six-week run. The shows have been incredible. The audiences have been extraordinary. Touring mental health is a serious and underaddressed issue in the music industry. ### LAST MINUTE CHANGES

**Section:** CAPTURE

**Archetype:** Terms Shifted After You Are Fully Committed

**Clinical name:** Moving Goalposts / Manufactured Dependency

The promoter who confirms your spot, collects your fee, then changes material terms after you are too invested to walk away without serious loss. Each change seems small. The cumulative impact is significant.

You confirmed spot 14 near the main entrance with electrical hookup in writing two months ago. ### CASH AND CARRY

### THE CONSIGNMENT TRAP

**Section:** CONTROL

**Archetype:** Work Left Behind, Payment Never Comes

**Clinical name:** Contractual Exploitation / Non-Payment

The shop, gallery, boutique, or pop-up that wants your work on consignment — and then does not pay, goes out of business with your inventory inside, or loses the reconciliation. Consignment is legitimate. Unprotected consignment is a gift.

"We would love to carry your line — we do 60/40 in your favor, you would be in great company." You drop off $1,800 of wholesale inventory. Three months later you are still waiting for the first check. The shop is still open. Your work is still on the shelves. Phone calls go unanswered. Emails get vague responses. Six months in you have recovered $600 of $1,800 in product and the shop just posted new arrivals on Instagram.

Consignment fraud is one of the most common forms of vendor exploitation because artists feel awkward being professional with people who seem enthusiastic about their work. The enthusiasm is real — your work is valuable. The payment intention varies. A consignment agreement is not distrust — it is the thing that makes the relationship honest and enforceable. Without it, recovering your inventory or your payment requires legal action that costs more than the inventory is worth.

**Tells:** No written consignment agreement with payment terms and reconciliation schedule / First payment arrives late or partial — this sets the pattern for everything that follows / No inventory tracking system or signed receipt for what you left / The owner becomes progressively harder to reach as time goes on

**Check:** M.A.R.K.E.T. — "M" CHECK: "Do I have a signed agreement with payment terms — or just a handshake and a hope?"

**The dark version (Consignment Agreement Minimum Terms):** Every consignment placement needs in writing: itemized list of pieces with your price and the split, payment schedule (monthly reconciliation is standard), what happens to unsold pieces and when (90-day return clause), who is responsible for lost or damaged inventory, and what access you have to check on your pieces. Bring a printed agreement to every drop-off and do not rely on theirs. If a shop will not sign your agreement — take your inventory back. That is your information about their payment intention.

**The move:** No signed consignment agreement, no inventory drop-off. This policy saves you every time.

**Say:** I use a standard consignment agreement for all my placements — I have one here for us both to sign before I leave anything.

**The truth:** Enthusiasm about your work is not a payment plan. A signed agreement is.

### YOUR DESIGN ON THEIR MERCH

**Section:** CONTROL

**Archetype:** Design Theft / Your Work Sold by Someone Else

**Clinical name:** Intellectual Property Theft / Copyright Violation

Your designs, your photographs, your product images appearing on someone else's booth, Etsy shop, or social media — without your permission, without credit, and often in direct competition with you on the same circuit.

A customer at your booth holds up their phone: "I saw something almost identical to this at the booth down the way — they are selling it for less." You go look. It is not almost identical. It IS identical — your photo, your design, printed by a drop-ship service. The vendor shrugs: "I found the image online, how was I supposed to know?" Your image has a watermark. It was cropped out. This is not an accident.

Design theft and image theft are endemic in the handmade marketplace — the internet makes copying easy and enforcement feels difficult. But copyright protection is automatic the moment you create original work and you have real options. The key is documentation: timestamp your original files, watermark everything you post publicly, maintain a creation file with original dated drafts. US Copyright registration ($65) enables enforcement with statutory damages of $750 to $30,000 per infringement.

**Tells:** Your product photos appearing on other vendors' social media without credit / Your designs appearing in physical or digital products from another seller / Drop-ship or print-on-demand versions of your work sold at lower prices on your circuit / Your business name or brand elements used by someone else in your market space

**Check:** M.A.R.K.E.T. — "R" CHECK: "Do I have timestamped documentation of my original designs that establishes my...

**The dark version (When Your Work Is Stolen — Your Options):** Documentation first: screenshot everything, note dates, save original files. DMCA takedown: file with the platform — Google, Etsy, and Instagram all have processes and take these seriously. Etsy: file an Intellectual Property infringement report — they will remove listings. Legal: small claims for amounts under your state limit. Copyright registration ($65 at copyright.gov) opens the door to statutory damages and attorney fees — making enforcement financially viable. Volunteer Lawyers for the Arts offers free consultations in most major cities.

**The move:** Watermark everything. Document your creation dates. Enforce your copyright.

**Say:** That is my image and my design. I need you to remove it from your inventory today. I have documentation of the original.

**The truth:** Your designs are copyrighted the moment you create them. You get to enforce that.

### BUYING AND SELLING SAFELY

### WHEN THEY DO NOT PAY

**Section:** TOOLS

**Archetype:** Your Options When Payment Does Not Come

You did the event. You delivered the inventory. You completed the commission. The payment did not come. This page covers what you actually do next — step by step — because most predatory promoters and buyers rely on vendors being too tired or too non-confrontational to pursue it.

It has been six weeks since the event. You have sent three polite emails. You have received two vague replies and one promise of "by end of week" that came and went. The promoter is still posting about upcoming events on Instagram. Your follow-up messages are being read but not replied to. You are owed $1,200 and you are running out of patience and confidence that it is coming.

Most non-payment situations resolve at the written demand stage because most bad-faith payers are not prepared to actually face legal consequences — they are relying on vendors giving up. A written demand with a specific deadline and a reference to your legal options is the trigger that moves most situations to resolution. The ones who still do not pay after a written demand are the ones you take to small claims. The process is simpler than most people think.

**Tells:** Payment delayed past agreed date without proactive communication from their side / Replies become progressively vague, slow, or one-word as time passes / "Accounting issues" or "cash flow" appear as explanations without a concrete resolution date / They are visibly active on social media and running their business while ignoring your payment

Step 2: small claims court — handles disputes up to $10,000-$25,000 depending on state, filing fee usually under $100, you represent yourself. Step 3: attorney demand letter for larger amounts ($150-300 one-time) frequently resolves it immediately. Step 4: publicizing non-payment in vendor communities is legal and effective. Document everything before going public. Most situations resolve at Step 1 or 2.

**The move:** Send the written demand. Most situations resolve there. Be prepared to file if they do not.

**Say:** Per our agreement, payment of [X] was due on [date]. Please remit by [new date] or I will pursue this through small claims court.

**The truth:** You did the work. You get paid. This is completely enforceable and simpler than they want you to think.

**Check:** H.O.L.D. — "O" CHECK: "Is the pattern of this person's engagement consistent with genuine support seeking — or...

**The move:** Trust the pattern. Name it to a colleague. Document it. That is what good safeguarding looks like.

**The truth:** Your care role requires discernment as much as compassion. Both are part of doing it well.

### BURNOUT AS A CONTROL TOOL

**Section:** CONDITION

**Archetype:** Being Kept Too Depleted to See Clearly

**Clinical name:** Resource Depletion / Control Through Exhaustion

The event structure or leadership that keeps staff and volunteers so busy, so sleep deprived, and so short on basic resources that the capacity to notice problems, raise concerns, or exercise independent judgment is systematically eliminated.

By Saturday afternoon you have been on since 6am, you have not had a proper meal, the staff communication channel is so full of urgent requests that you have stopped reading it, and the supervisor you would report concerns to is also clearly running on empty. Something happened in the camping area last night that three people have mentioned in passing but nobody has formally reported. You know you should do something about it. You genuinely do not have the capacity to figure out what.

Systematic depletion of staff and volunteers is sometimes accidental and sometimes deliberate. When everyone is too exhausted to notice patterns or raise concerns — problems that would otherwise be caught and addressed do not get caught and addressed. If you are in an event structure where your basic needs are not being met and there is no functional path for raising concerns — that is information about the organization, not just about the event. Name it. Document it. And protect yourself accordingly.

**Tells:** Staffing is so thin that there is never a moment to observe, think, or raise concerns / Basic needs — food, sleep, breaks — are systematically not being met for staff and volunteers / Reporting channels are so overwhelmed that nothing non-urgent gets processed / Leadership is also depleted — there is no functional escalation path for concerns

**Check:** H.O.L.D. — "H" CHECK: "Do I have the basic resources I need to do this role safely — food, sleep, breaks, a...

**The move:** Meet your own basic needs first. You cannot report-effectively from a place of complete depletion.

### DON'T RUIN THE VIBE OF THE EVENT

**Section:** CONDITION

**Archetype:** Reporting Suppression / Don't Be the Problem

**Clinical name:** Tone Policing / Reporting Suppression

The direct or indirect pressure on staff and volunteers not to formally report incidents because it would create problems for the event, upset attendees, attract unwanted attention, or disrupt the experience. Your report becomes the problem rather than the incident.

You witnessed something between two attendees in the camping area that you are fairly sure was not consensual. You reported it to your supervisor. She took it seriously in the moment. Can we just make sure the people involved are okay and handle this internally?" You feel the pressure of a hundred things at once.

Reporting suppression protects the organization and the event at the expense of the people harmed. When incidents are handled informally at the request of event leadership — no report, no documentation, no formal process — the people harmed lose their ability to seek redress, the pattern goes untracked, and the organization avoids accountability. Your role is to report. The decision about how to handle the report is not yours alone. Make the report and let the process work.

**Tells:** Informal resolution is proposed for situations that require formal reporting / "The event" or "the community" invoked as reasons not to formally document an incident / You are asked to handle it yourself rather than escalating through proper channels / Pressure to wait, consider, or discuss rather than document and report immediately

**The dark version (What To Do When Told Not To Report):** If you are asked not to formally report an incident: (1) Document the incident yourself in writing regardless — date, time, location, what you witnessed, what you reported, and what response you received including the instruction not to formally report. (2) If the incident involves assault, harassment, or a crime — you may have independent legal obligations to report depending on your role and jurisdiction. (3) If the organization is suppressing reports: that is reportable to relevant authorities and professional bodies after the event. (4) RAINN (rainn.org) has guidance for bystanders and witnesses as well as victims.

**Say:** I hear you and I understand the concern — I am still going to document this formally. I will send it to you in writing.

**The truth:** Reporting harm is not drama. It is the job. Anyone who tells you otherwise is protecting the wrong thing.

Your supervisor has been warm and supportive all weekend. He has also made several comments that felt personal rather than professional. **The move:** Document it. Report it through the highest available channel. Your experience in this role should not cost you this.

### THIS IS YOUR FAULT

**Section:** CONTROL

**Archetype:** Being Blamed When Something Goes Wrong on Your Watch

**Clinical name:** Scapegoating / Accountability Deflection

The organizational dynamic where front-line staff and volunteers are held responsible for systemic failures — inadequate staffing, poor training, unclear protocols — that were set in motion by leadership decisions long before your shift began.

An incident happened during your shift. You responded as best you could with the training and resources you had. Afterward, the event director calls you in and the tone is very different from the supportive one you experienced all weekend. Suddenly the focus is on what you did or did not do. The staffing shortage that left you covering an area alone, the training gaps, the protocol that was unclear — none of that is part of the conversation. You are.

Front-line workers are the easiest people to blame for systemic failures because they were physically present when things went wrong. Organizations that do not want to examine their own structural failures direct accountability downward onto the people least able to defend themselves. You are not required to accept blame for conditions you did not create. Document your version of events in writing immediately after any incident. Do not sign anything you do not fully understand and agree with.

**Tells:** Post-incident conversation focuses exclusively on your actions rather than systemic conditions / Systemic failures — understaffing, training gaps, resource shortfalls — are not part of the review / You are asked to sign statements or acknowledgments that implicitly accept responsibility / The framing shifts from "what happened" to "what did you do" in ways that feel like assignment of blame

**Check:** H.O.L.D. — "L" CHECK: "Have I documented my version of events in writing immediately — before organizational...

**The dark version (Protecting Yourself After An Incident):** Immediately after any significant incident: (1) Write your own account in as much detail as possible — what you observed, what you did, what resources and training you had, what the conditions were. (3) If asked to participate in a formal review: you have the right to have someone present with you. (4) If you feel you are being scapegoated for systemic failures: contact a lawyer or the National Lawyers Guild before signing anything.

**The move:** Write your own account immediately. Do not sign anything before reading it carefully against your own record.

**Say:** I want to cooperate fully with any review — I am also going to document my own account of what happened. Can I have until tomorrow to do that?

**The truth:** You are responsible for your actions. You are not responsible for the systemic conditions you were placed in.

### BYSTANDER ACTIVATION FOR STAFF

**Section:** CONTROL

**Archetype:** What You Do In The Moment You See Something

**Clinical name:** Active Bystander Intervention / Safeguarding

As staff or a volunteer you have visibility, authority, and access that most attendees do not. You also have the specific responsibility to use it. This page is about what that looks like in practice — not in theory.

You are doing a walk-through of the camping area mid-afternoon and you see a woman sitting with a man who seems agitated. Her body language is closed and she is looking at the ground. You are in your staff vest. You have radio access. You have the authority to intervene in ways that most attendees do not. You also have the very human impulse to not want to make it worse, not want to assume wrong, not want to create a scene. The impulse is real. So is your responsibility.

Staff and volunteers have a fundamentally different role in bystander intervention than attendees — you have authority, visibility, backup, and a professional obligation to act that most attendees do not. The threshold for intervention is lower for you. The tools available are better. The accountability for not acting is higher. See something, do something — and document what you did and why.

**Tells:** Your staff vest and radio change the social dynamics of an intervention — use that / You can ask directly: "Is everything okay here?" — your role gives you standing to ask / You can radio for support before approaching — you do not have to handle it alone / Documentation after any intervention is part of the job, not optional

**Check:** H.O.L.D. — "O" CHECK: "What am I observing right now and what does my role require me to do with that...

**The dark version (Staff Bystander Toolkit):** What staff have that attendees do not: (1) Radio — you can call for backup before approaching. Use it. (2) Authority — "Is everything okay here?" from someone in a staff vest lands differently than the same question from an attendee. (3) Known safe spaces — you know where Harm Reduction and medical are. You can offer to walk someone there. (4) Incident reporting — everything you witness and act on gets documented. That documentation protects people, creates patterns, and holds the organization accountable. (5) Colleagues — you are not alone. Use your team.

**The move:** Use your radio, your authority, your access, and your colleagues. You have more tools than anyone else here.

**Say:** "Hey — I am staff. Is everything okay here?" Then radio for support if needed.

**The truth:** Your vest and your radio are the tools for this moment. Use them.

### YOUR RIGHTS AS A VOLUNTEER

**Section:** TOOLS

**Archetype:** What You Can Refuse and What You Are Owed

**Clinical name:** Volunteer Rights / Labor Awareness

Volunteers have rights. Not the same rights as employees in every jurisdiction but real rights nonetheless — the right to safe working conditions, the right to refuse unsafe tasks, the right to leave a role that is causing harm, and the right to documentation of what you were promised.

You are being asked to cover a solo overnight shift in a remote area of the festival with no radio contact and no clear protocol for what to do if something happens. Your gut says this is not safe. Your supervisor says everyone does it and there is nobody else. You want to be helpful. You also want to go home in one piece. The question is whether you have the right to say no — and the answer is yes.

Festival volunteer culture often blurs the line between genuine community contribution and labor exploitation. The "we are all in this together" framing can make it feel selfish or disloyal to refuse unsafe tasks or insist on basic working conditions. It is not. Volunteers who are placed in unsafe conditions and something goes wrong face real consequences — personal harm, trauma, and sometimes legal exposure. You have the right to refuse conditions that put you at unreasonable risk.

**Tells:** Being asked to work alone in unsafe conditions without adequate communication or support / Being asked to cover tasks that are outside your training, role description, or competency / Being pressured to continue working when you have raised a safety concern about your conditions / Basic needs — food, water, breaks, sleep — being systematically denied for operational reasons

**Check:** H.O.L.D. — "H" CHECK: "Am I being asked to do something that my training, my safety, or my basic needs require...

**The dark version (Volunteer Rights In Practice):** In practice, volunteer rights include: the right to know what your role involves before you commit, the right to refuse tasks outside your role description or competency without losing your position, the right to safe working conditions including adequate communication, support, and backup, the right to leave a role that is actively harmful to you without legal consequence in most jurisdictions, and the right to documentation of what was agreed. After the event: formal feedback to the organization and to any bodies that oversee the event. Your experience matters and your feedback improves conditions for future volunteers.

**The move:** Know your rights before you need them. Refusing unsafe conditions is not abandonment — it is professional judgment.

**Say:** "I am not comfortable covering that alone without radio contact — what backup can you provide or what is the alternative?"

**The truth:** Saying no to an unsafe task is not letting the team down. It is doing the job correctly.

### THE DEBRIEF IS PART OF THE JOB

**Section:** TOOLS

**Archetype:** Processing After a Hard Shift or Difficult Incident

**Clinical name:** Post-Incident Debrief / Secondary Trauma Prevention

Festival caregiving roles expose you to a concentration of difficult human experiences in a very short time. Decompressing from that exposure is not optional — it is what prevents the experiences you carry home from becoming experiences that stay with you.

You supported an overdose response, two assault disclosures, and a mental health crisis in the same shift. All of them resolved as well as they could. You did your job well. It is now Sunday evening and the festival is winding down and you are sitting in your car in the parking area before driving home and you realize you have not actually stopped moving since Thursday. You are not sure what you feel. You are not sure you feel anything. That numbness is the thing to pay attention to.

Secondary traumatic stress — also called vicarious trauma — is a recognized occupational hazard for people in caregiving and emergency response roles. It develops from repeated exposure to other people's traumatic experiences. Festival caregiving compresses this exposure dramatically. Debrief is the professional standard for managing it — named processing of what you experienced, with colleagues or a professional, before it gets metabolized as unprocessed trauma.

**Tells:** Emotional numbness or flatness after a shift involving difficult incidents / Intrusive mental replaying of difficult situations you witnessed or supported / Difficulty transitioning out of the staff role back into your regular self / Physical symptoms — tension, headache, fatigue — that persist well after your shift ends

**Check:** H.O.L.D. — "D" CHECK: "Have I actually stopped and processed what I experienced today — or am I just pushing...

**The dark version (What An Effective Debrief Looks Like):** At minimum after any difficult shift: 15-30 minutes of quiet transition before driving or going to the main festival. Name — out loud to at least one colleague — the most difficult thing you experienced this shift. After the event: talk to at least one person outside the festival context about what you carried. If multiple difficult incidents happened: consider a session with a therapist who works with first responders or emergency services. Resources specifically for caregivers: Zendo Project (zendoproject.org) trains staff and can refer to ongoing support. Psychology Today (psychologytoday.com) has therapist finders by specialty.

**The move:** Name what you experienced out loud to at least one colleague before leaving. That is the minimum.

**Say:** Before we all head out — can we take ten minutes just to name what today was like? I need to say some of it out loud.

**The truth:** The experiences you process become wisdom. The ones you do not process become weight. Debrief is how you choose which is which.

### YOUR ACCESS IS NOT YOUR PERMISSION

**Section:** ACCOUNTABILITY

**Archetype:** Using Your Role Responsibly

Staff and volunteer roles at festivals come with access — to sensitive information, to vulnerable people, to spaces and resources that most attendees do not have. This page is about using that access in ways that are genuinely in service of the people you are there to protect.

You are a Ranger and someone came to you in genuine distress about a situation in their camp. You helped them, you supported them, you were exactly what the role is supposed to be. You also now know specific private information about them — who they were with, what happened, where their camp is, what their vulnerable moment looked like. The question is not whether that information is real. The question is what you do with it after they walk away from your care.

The access that comes with festival staff and volunteer roles creates real power over people who are in vulnerable moments. That power is a tool for service — it is not a benefit of the role. Information shared in a care context is confidential. Access to spaces and resources is role-specific. Ongoing personal contact with people you supported in your role raises the same issues as any therapist-client boundary — the power imbalance does not disappear when the immediate support need does.

**Tells:** Information shared with you in a care context belongs to the person who shared it — not to you / Access to the festival layout, staff areas, and resources is for the role — not for personal use / Ongoing contact with people you supported in your role is a boundary issue in most contexts / The power of your role continues to exist even when you are off duty at the same event

**Check:** H.O.L.D. — "H" CHECK: "Am I using my access in service of the people I am there to protect — or for my own...

**The dark version (The Ethics Of Your Access):** Ethical use of staff access: information shared with you in a care context stays in the care context — it does not get shared with other staff except on a need-to-know basis and not at all outside the organizational context. Physical access to secure or restricted areas is for role purposes only. Contact with people you supported ends when the support need ends — seeking ongoing personal contact is a boundary violation regardless of how the connection felt. If you are unsure whether something is within bounds: ask your supervisor before doing it, not after.

**The move:** Use your access for the people you are there to serve. Full stop.

**Say:** "I want to check before I do this — is this within the scope of my role?"

**The truth:** The access is not yours. It belongs to the role. Use it accordingly.

### STAFF CULTURE THAT PROTECTS PEOPLE

**Section:** ACCOUNTABILITY

**Archetype:** What Healthy Staff Culture Actually Looks Like

The culture of a festival's staff team is one of the most important determinants of how safe the event actually is for everyone. This page is about what healthy staff culture looks like — and how to build it from wherever you are in the organizational structure.

The briefing before gates open sets the tone. The lead says: "Our job this weekend is to hold the space so everyone can have the experience they came for. That means we take every concern seriously, we report what we see, we take care of each other so we can take care of everyone else, and we never dismiss a gut feeling. If something feels off — name it to your team. There are no bad reports. There are only missed patterns." That briefing changes how the whole weekend goes.

Healthy staff culture is built from the first briefing and maintained through how leadership responds to the first concern that is raised. Organizations where leadership takes concerns seriously, supports staff wellbeing, and holds itself to the same standards it holds front-line workers create genuinely safer events. Organizations that suppress concerns, burn out volunteers, and deflect accountability downward create events where harm is more likely and less likely to be addressed.

**Tells:** Leadership explicitly states that all concerns will be taken seriously — not managed down / Staff are encouraged to name gut feelings and concerns without requiring certainty / Debrief and staff wellbeing are treated as operational priorities not afterthoughts / Accountability runs in all directions — leadership is held to the same standards as front-line staff

**Check:** H.O.L.D. — "O" CHECK: "Does the culture of this staff team make it easy or hard to raise concerns? That tells you a...

**The dark version (How To Build Healthy Staff Culture From Your Position):** Regardless of where you sit in the organization: (1) Model taking gut feelings seriously by naming yours out loud. "Something about that felt off to me — I am going to document it." (2) Celebrate colleagues who raise concerns rather than treating them as troublemakers. (3) Debrief after difficult shifts even when leadership does not require it. (4) Give honest post-event feedback to the organization about what worked and what did not. (5) If you are in leadership: the first time a concern is dismissed sets the culture for every concern that follows. Take them all seriously.

**The move:** Build the culture you want to work in. It starts with how you respond to the first concern you hear.

**Say:** I want to hear about that — tell me what you saw and let us figure out together what to do with it.

**The truth:** The safest events are the ones with staff teams who look out for each other as fiercely as they look out for attendees.

### THE DEBRIEF PROTOCOL

**Section:** ACCOUNTABILITY

**Archetype:** Processing What You Carry So It Does Not Carry You

**Clinical name:** Post-Incident Debrief / Secondary Trauma Prevention

Debrief is not extra. Debrief is part of the job. What you name and process from a difficult shift becomes wisdom. What you do not name becomes weight you carry into the next shift and beyond.

The shift ends and the tendency is to immediately transition back to the festival or to crash into sleep. The transition period — the ten to thirty minutes between the end of a shift and the next thing — is when the debrief happens. Not in a formal structured way necessarily. Just: what was the hardest thing from this shift, said out loud, to at least one colleague. That is the minimum. And it changes everything about what you carry out.

Secondary traumatic stress accumulates when difficult experiences are not processed. The processing does not have to be elaborate — naming what was hard, in plain language, to someone who was also there or who understands the context, is often sufficient for immediate decompression. It is the naming that matters. What stays unnamed stays active. Harm Reduction volunteers who debrief consistently report lower rates of burnout and secondary trauma than those who do not.

**Tells:** Name the hardest thing from the shift out loud to at least one colleague before leaving / Physical transition: change out of your shift vest, eat something, drink water / If multiple difficult incidents happened: 20-30 minutes of quiet before re-entering the festival / Weekly or post-event check-in with a mental health professional if this is regular work for you

**Check:** C.A.R.E. — "E" CHECK: "Have I actually stopped and named what I am carrying from this shift — or am I pushing...

**The move:** Name what was hard. Out loud. To someone. Before you leave the tent. That is the minimum.

**Say:** Before we all head out — can we take ten minutes to say what today was like? I need to name some of it out loud.

**The truth:** What you name, you can put down. What you do not name, you carry. Name it.

The event cost more than projected. The attendance was lower than you hoped. The performer you agreed to pay $2,000 is now getting a message that you can only do $1,200 "given how the event went." She had no say in how the event went. She delivered her performance. She incurred the travel costs. She fulfilled her side of the agreement. The event's financial performance is your risk, not hers.

Late payment and post-event fee adjustments are so normalized in the independent event world that many promoters do not register them as the contract violations they are. They are. When you agree to a fee and a payment timeline, that agreement is binding. The event's financial performance does not change your obligation. Artists and vendors who absorb your losses are subsidizing your events with their own labor and financial risk. That is not a sustainable or ethical business model.

**Tells:** Post-event financial "adjustments" are contract violations — not conversations to negotiate / The event's costs and revenue are your business risk — not a risk that transfers to your artists / Payment timelines in contracts are enforceable — "end of month" is a legal commitment / Offering future booking as compensation for current non-payment is not compensation

**Check:** E.V.E.N.T. — "T" CHECK: "Am I paying what I agreed, to whom I agreed, on the timeline I agreed — every time?"

**The dark version (What Professional Payment Practice Looks Like):** Professional payment practice: (1) Pay deposits on the agreed timeline before the event — not when you get around to it. (2) Reconcile and pay final amounts within the agreed timeframe after the event — 7-14 days is standard. (3) If you genuinely cannot pay on time: communicate proactively before the deadline, not after you have already missed it. (4) Do not offer future booking, exposure, or "a better deal next time" as substitutes for agreed payment. (5) If you had to adjust fees due to genuine crisis: negotiate BEFORE the event, not after. Late adjustment is a unilateral contract change. That is not how agreements work.

**The move:** Pay what you agreed, on the agreed timeline. Every time. This is the whole standard.

**Say:** "The payment will be in your account by [agreed date]. Let me know if you do not receive it by then."

**The truth:** Your financial reputation is built one payment at a time. It is also destroyed one payment at a time.

### YOUR STAFF AND VOLUNTEERS

**Section:** RESPONSIBILITY

**Archetype:** What You Owe the People Who Run Your Event

Your event runs because people show up to make it run. Staff and volunteers who work your event are owed specific things — clear terms, safe working conditions, basic resources, and the knowledge that raising concerns will be taken seriously rather than cost them their position.

Your volunteer briefing says "this is a family" and "we are all in this together." It does not say what the actual shift hours are, what accommodation is provided, what meals are covered, what happens if someone needs to leave early, or how to raise a concern about another staff member. By Saturday your Harm Reduction team is short-staffed, two volunteers have left without explanation, and a concern that was raised Friday morning has not been acted on. The family framing did not substitute for the operational clarity that was needed.

Festival promoters who treat staff and volunteers as family while providing no operational clarity are using the emotional frame to avoid the practical obligations. Family framing does not substitute for written agreements. Community language does not provide food and rest. And a culture where concerns are raised but not acted on is a culture where the next concern does not get raised — until something goes seriously wrong.

**Tells:** Volunteer agreements should specify hours, accommodation, meals, and exit terms in writing / Staff should have a clear reporting process for concerns about other staff members / Basic needs — food, water, breaks, sleep — should be structurally provided not assumed / Concerns raised by staff should be documented and acted on within a defined timeframe

**Check:** E.V.E.N.T. — "N" CHECK: "Do my staff have what they need to do their jobs safely — and do they have a clear path...

**The dark version (Staff And Volunteer Minimum Standards):** For every event: (1) Written volunteer agreements specifying hours, accommodation, meals, and exit terms before the event. (2) Pre-event briefing covering reporting process, code words, daily check-in expectations. (3) Designated staff welfare role — someone whose job includes checking that staff are okay. (4) Documented process for raising concerns about other staff members that does not require going to their direct supervisor. (5) Post-event debrief for staff that includes honest assessment of what could be improved. These are not extras. They are the baseline of running an event with people in it.

**The move:** Provide clear written terms, safe conditions, and a functioning reporting process for every person who works your event.

**Say:** Here is your written volunteer agreement — read it carefully. Here is how to raise concerns. Here is what I am committed to providing.

**The truth:** Your event runs on the labor of other people. You owe them clarity, safety, and the knowledge that concerns will be heard.

### ENFORCE YOUR OWN POLICIES

**Section:** RESPONSIBILITY

**Archetype:** Consent Culture That Is Real, Not Decorative

Many events have consent policies. Fewer events enforce them consistently. The gap between a stated policy and its enforcement is not just a failure of follow-through — it is the active message that the policy is decorative rather than real.

Your event's consent policy is posted at the entrance. It is in your promotional materials. Your team briefing included it. On Saturday night a security staff member reports a consent violation to the team lead. The team lead, who knows the person being reported, decides to handle it informally. The person stays at the event. By Sunday morning two more people have had experiences with the same individual. The policy did not fail. The enforcement did.

Inconsistent enforcement of consent policies does more damage than no policy at all — because it signals to potential violators that the policy is navigable through the right relationships, and to potential reporters that reporting may not be worth the cost. Consistent enforcement regardless of who the violator is — including people who are connected, contributing, or beloved — is the only thing that makes the policy real. The first time you fail to enforce it is the moment you define what the policy actually is.

**Tells:** Consent violations handled informally when formal processes exist / Known violators who remain at events because of their connections or contributions / Different enforcement standards applied based on who the violator is / Reports that are received and not acted on within a defined timeframe

**Check:** E.V.E.N.T. — "E" CHECK: "Am I enforcing my consent policy consistently — regardless of who the violator is?"

**The dark version (What Real Consent Policy Enforcement Looks Like):** Real enforcement means: (1) Every reported consent violation is documented and reviewed by a designated safety lead — not handled informally by whoever is available. (2) The safety lead has actual authority to remove people from the event. (3) Removal decisions are made based on the report, not on the violator's connections or contributions. (4) The person who made the report is updated on what happened. (5) Patterns are tracked — one report about a person is an incident, two is a pattern that changes the response. (6) Post-event review includes analysis of consent-related reports and how they were handled. This is what enforcement looks like. Anything less is decoration.

**The move:** Enforce your consent policy consistently. The first exception defines what the policy actually is.

**Say:** This person has been reported for a consent violation. Here is our process and here is what happens next regardless of who they are.

**The truth:** A consent policy you do not enforce is not a consent policy. It is a liability.

### THE RECORDING RIGHTS QUESTION

**Section:** STANDARDS

**Archetype:** What You Actually Own From Your Event

Your event produces content — video, audio, photography, social media. The question of what you own and what your artists and performers own is both a legal question and an ethical one. This page is about being honest about both.

Your festival set videos are performing well on your YouTube channel. They are driving discovery for your next event. They are also generating streaming revenue through the platform's music content system. The artists in those videos were paid for their performance. They were not paid for the recording, the distribution, or the streaming revenue. Your standard booking confirmation contained a rights clause they did not negotiate. The content that is building your brand was created by people who are not sharing in its returns.

The gap between what promoters legally own through standard contracts and what they ethically owe the artists who created the content is significant. Artists whose performances are recorded, distributed, and monetized are entitled to participate in that revenue. A recording rights clause buried in a booking confirmation is not the same as a negotiated agreement. The ethical standard is transparency about what you are doing with the content and fair participation for the people who created it.

**Tells:** Sweeping recording rights clauses in standard booking confirmation language / Live recordings generating streaming or licensing revenue without artist participation / Photography and video of artists used in commercial contexts without their agreement / Social media content featuring artists used to promote future events without compensation

**Check:** E.V.E.N.T. — "V" CHECK: "Are the artists whose recordings I am using being compensated fairly for the commercial...

**The dark version (Ethical Recording Rights Practice):** Ethical practice for event recordings: (1) Be explicit in booking agreements about what recording will happen and for what purpose. (2) Distinguish between archival recording (no commercial use) and commercial recording (streaming, licensing, promotion) — these are different agreements. (3) Share streaming and licensing revenue with performing artists — this is both the legal standard in many contexts and the ethical one in all contexts. (4) Use artist images and recordings for event promotion only with their explicit agreement. (5) When in doubt: ask before you use. The conversation is easier before the content is published than after.

**The move:** Be transparent about what you record, how you use it, and share revenue with the people who created it.

**Say:** Here is what we record at our events and how we use it. Here is how we share revenue with performing artists.

**The truth:** The content that builds your brand was created by artists. Treat their contribution accordingly.

**Clinical name:** Professional Ethics / Operational Standards

This page is the practical one. Specific practices and standards that distinguish events built on ethical foundations from ones built on the exploitation of artists, vendors, staff, and attendees.

The promoters you respect — the ones whose names on a lineup or a market mean something — have all built reputations over time through the same things: they pay what they agreed. **The move:** Run the checklist before and after every event. The reputation you build is the one that is accurate.

**Say:** Here is our event agreement. Here is our safety plan. Here is how to raise concerns. Here is when payment will arrive.

### WATER BEFORE YOU ARE THIRSTY

**Section:** HYDRATION

**Archetype:** Hydration, Electrolytes, and Heat Safety

**Clinical name:** Heat Illness Prevention / Hydration Protocol

By the time you feel thirsty at a festival you are already behind. The combination of dancing, heat, sun exposure, and many substances means your hydration needs are significantly higher than your normal daily baseline — and your body's thirst signal is often suppressed or delayed.

It is 2pm Saturday and it is hot. You have been dancing since 11am. You have had one bottle of water and three sips from someone else's hydration pack. You feel fine — energized actually, slightly euphoric. That feeling is not a reliable indicator of your hydration status. By the time the headache hits and you start feeling off your balance you are already significantly behind. The medical tent is about to get busy because you are not the only one.

Festivals combine multiple dehydration risk factors simultaneously: physical exertion from dancing, sun and heat exposure, alcohol and stimulant use (both increase fluid loss), and environments that make stopping to drink feel inconvenient. Heat exhaustion is common and treatable. Heat stroke is a medical emergency that kills people. The difference between them is speed of treatment. Know the signs. Act early. The medical tent is there specifically for this.

**Tells:** Drink water consistently throughout the day — at least 500ml per hour in heat and while dancing / Electrolytes matter as much as water — plain water alone can cause hyponatremia in high volumes / Signs of heat exhaustion: heavy sweating, cool pale skin, weakness, nausea, headache / Signs of heat stroke — EMERGENCY: hot red dry skin, no sweating, confusion, loss of consciousness

**Check:** S.A.F.E. — "F" CHECK: "Do I know where the medical tent is — and have I actually had enough water in the last hour?"

**The dark version (Heat Stroke Is A Medical Emergency):** Heat stroke signs: hot, red, dry skin with no sweating. Confusion, slurred speech, loss of consciousness. Body temperature above 104F. This is life-threatening and requires immediate medical attention. What to do right now: call for medical help immediately, move the person to shade and cool them with water or ice packs to the neck, armpits, and groin, do not give fluids to someone who is confused or unconscious. Festival medical teams are trained for this. Get them immediately. Do not wait to see if it gets better.

**The move:** Drink water now. Take an electrolyte packet. Find shade for ten minutes. Do it before you need to.

**Say:** Hey — when did you last have water? Come on, let us both drink something right now.

**The truth:** Every medical tent at every festival sees preventable heat illness every day. Do not be that story.

### FOOD, SLEEP, AND YOUR ACTUAL BODY

**Section:** HYDRATION

**Archetype:** The Physical Infrastructure That Makes Everything Safer

**Clinical name:** Physical Resilience / Basic Needs Protocol

Festivals are designed for endurance — multiple days, extended hours, high stimulation. Your body has limits regardless of how good the music is and how much you want to keep going. The people who have the best full-weekend experiences are the ones who treat their physical needs as seriously as their social ones.

It is Sunday morning. You have slept a total of six hours over three nights. You have eaten intermittently and mostly festival food. You have consumed substances that have both depleted your body and masked the signals it is trying to send you. You are running on a combination of adrenaline, social excitement, and genuine will. You feel okay. You are not okay. And the decisions you make today — about substances, about consent, about safety — will be made from this state.

Sleep deprivation is one of the most consistently underestimated risk factors at festivals. After 36 hours without sleep, cognitive impairment is equivalent to a 0.10 blood alcohol level. After 48 hours it is significantly higher. Combined with substances, heat, and social pressure this creates a state where the very judgment systems you need to make good decisions about your own safety are significantly compromised. Rest is not weakness. Rest is strategy.

**Tells:** Eat at least two real meals per day even when the music is calling — your judgment depends on it / Sleep deprivation after 36 hours significantly impairs decision-making even when you feel fine / Sunscreen reapplication every two hours — sunburn on top of everything else is genuinely dangerous / Foot care matters — blisters become infections, infections at festivals are hard to treat

**Check:** S.A.F.E. — "A" CHECK: "Am I making decisions right now with my full judgment — or with a significantly depleted...

**The dark version (The Case For Actually Sleeping):** You will remember more of your festival if you sleep. Genuine experiences integrate better when your brain is functioning. You will be more present in conversations, more able to receive music, more connected to the people around you. The FOMO of missing a few hours by sleeping is almost always outweighed by the quality of the hours you are present for. Plan for at least one solid sleep block in a 72-hour event. Bring earplugs and an eye mask. Tell your campmates you are sleeping and not to disturb you. This is not weakness. This is how you have the festival you actually came for.

**The move:** Eat something real. Sleep when you can. Your experience of the festival depends on your body being functional.

**Say:** I am going to get a couple hours — wake me up for the set I marked. I will be more here for it if I sleep.

**The truth:** Rest is not missing the festival. Rest is what makes you available to actually be in it.

### TEST EVERYTHING EVERY TIME

**Section:** SUBSTANCES

**Archetype:** Fentanyl Strips, Reagent Kits, and Why This Is Non-Optional

**Clinical name:** Harm Reduction Protocol / Substance Testing

Fentanyl is in the supply. It is in pills, powders, and substances where people are not expecting it. It is invisible, odorless, and a lethal dose is smaller than a few grains of salt. Testing is the single most effective harm reduction step you can take.

"Someone in our camp tested their MDMA Saturday morning and it came back as something completely different." This happens at every major festival. Not occasionally — every festival, every season. The person who handed it to you may not have known either. Testing is not about distrust of the person who gave it to you. It is about knowing what is actually in what you are consuming. You would not eat a wild mushroom without identifying it first. Same exact energy. Same exact stakes.

The recreational drug supply has been contaminated with fentanyl and fentanyl analogues at a scale that makes testing not optional — it is a basic safety step equivalent to checking that your tent stakes are in the ground before it rains.

**Tells:** Fentanyl test strips: dissolve a tiny amount in water, dip — ONE line means fentanyl present, do not use / Reagent kits (Marquis, Mecke, Simon's): color reactions identify MDMA, MDA, amphetamines, and adulterants / Start with a much smaller amount than you think is the dose — festival environments intensify everything / Wait before redosing — onset times vary and stacking doses before you feel the first one is how people get in trouble

**Check:** S.A.F.E. — "A"CHECK: "Have I tested this — and does everyone in my group know what to do if something goes...

**The move:** Test your stuff. Carry Narcan. Know the overdose signs. These three things save lives.

**Say:** Hold on — let me test it first. I have strips in my kit. Two minutes.

**The truth:** The two minutes it takes to test is not inconvenience. It is the difference between a good night and a medical emergency.

### DANGEROUS COMBINATIONS

**Section:** SUBSTANCES

**Archetype:** What Not to Mix and Why It Matters

**Clinical name:** Drug Interaction / Polydrug Risk

Most serious adverse events at festivals involve combinations of substances rather than any single substance. Festival environments where multiple substances are available and social pressure normalizes consumption make accidental dangerous combinations common.

You took MDMA earlier. Someone offers you something they describe as a downer to take the edge off. You do not know exactly what it is. You are already altered and the offer feels casual and the person seems trustworthy. This specific combination — a stimulant and a CNS depressant — is one of the most common contributors to serious adverse events at festivals. Not because either thing alone is necessarily fatal but because the combination creates unpredictable interactions that your altered state makes it hard to assess.

Polydrug use — using more than one substance — is extremely common at festivals and the risk profile of combinations is not intuitively obvious. Substances that seem to balance each other out are often actually masking warning signals from your body. The feeling of being okay is not reliable information when two substances are creating competing effects. The Harm Reduction tent has staff trained in polydrug risk assessment. When in doubt — go there before you take something else.

**Tells:** MDMA plus alcohol: dramatically increases dehydration and cardiovascular strain / Stimulants plus depressants (including alcohol): mask each other's effects and create unpredictable states / MDMA plus other serotonergic substances: risk of serotonin syndrome which can be life-threatening / Any substance plus another substance from an unknown or unverified source: compounded unknown risk

**Check:** S.A.F.E. — "A" CHECK: "Do I know what I have already taken and do I actually know what I am being offered right...

**The dark version (Harm Reduction Tent — Use Them For This):** Harm Reduction tents at major festivals have staff trained in substance interaction risk. You can walk in and say "I took X a few hours ago and someone is offering me Y — is that a problem?" and receive factual, non-judgmental information. This is confidential. They are not the police. They are not going to take away your substances or report you. They are there specifically to help you make safer choices. TripSit (tripsit.me) also has a free online drug combination checker that works on mobile. Check before you mix.

**The move:** Know what you have taken before taking anything else. When unsure — ask the Harm Reduction tent.

**Say:** What is that exactly? I need to know before I decide — I already took something earlier.

**The truth:** The combination is where most of the risk lives. Know what you are mixing before you mix it.

### WHEN SOMEONE IS IN TROUBLE

**Section:** SUBSTANCES

**Archetype:** Overdose Response and Getting Help Without Fear

**Clinical name:** Overdose Response / Emergency Protocol

The most important thing to Know about someone in trouble from substances is that you call for help immediately — and that Good Samaritan laws in most US states protect you from prosecution when you do. The call saves the life. The delay kills.

Your campmate is not responsive. You called their name. You shook their shoulder. They are breathing but slowly — it sounds wrong. Their lips look slightly blue. You do not know exactly what they took. You are scared. You are also the person standing there right now and you are the only thing standing between this situation and a very different outcome. You have about two minutes to make a decision that matters enormously.

Opioid overdose is the most immediately life-threatening substance emergency at festivals and Narcan reverses it. But stimulant overdose, serotonin syndrome, and heat stroke can also present as altered consciousness and require different emergency responses. The common thread is this: if someone is unresponsive or their breathing is wrong — call for medical help immediately and administer Narcan if you have it, because if it is an opioid overdose the Narcan will help and if it is not it will not harm them.

**Tells:** Unresponsive or very difficult to rouse — this is the clearest emergency sign / Slow, shallow, or stopped breathing — count breaths if unsure, fewer than one per 5 seconds is critical / Blue or purple tinge to lips or fingertips — indicates oxygen deprivation / Gurgling or snoring sounds from airway — roll them to recovery position on their side immediately

**Check:** S.A.F.E. — "F" CHECK: "Do I know where the medical tent is and do I have Narcan in my camp right now?"

**The dark version (The 60-Second Overdose Response Protocol):** Step 1: Try to rouse them — call their name, rub knuckles firmly on their sternum. Step 2: Call for medical help immediately — festival medics, 911, anyone with a radio. Do this NOW, do not wait. Step 3: If you have Narcan — one puff in each nostril. Repeat in 2-3 minutes if no response. Step 4: Roll them into recovery position on their side so they do not choke on vomit. Step 5: Stay with them until medical help arrives. Good Samaritan laws protect you. Making the call is always the right choice. Always.

**The move:** Call for help first. Narcan second. Recovery position third. Stay with them. Do not leave.

**Say:** "Someone is unresponsive — I need medical help at [your location] right now!" Then do not hang up.

**The truth:** The call you make in the next 60 seconds is the difference. Make it.

### SEE SOMETHING — DO SOMETHING

**Section:** SEE SOMETHING

**Archetype:** How to Actually Intervene When Something Looks Wrong

**Clinical name:** Active Bystander Intervention

At some point this weekend you will see something that does not look right. A situation. A person. A dynamic. The bystander effect is real — in groups, individuals are less likely to act because everyone assumes someone else will. You are the someone else.

You are walking between stages and you see a woman sitting with a man who seems agitated. Her body language is closed. He is leaning into her space and she is looking at the ground. You are not sure if it is anything. You almost keep walking because you do not want to assume, do not want to make it weird, do not want to be wrong. But something caught. This is the moment. This is the one. You have three options and they all take less than two minutes.

The most effective bystander interventions are the subtle ones — the distraction approach changes the social dynamic without confrontation and gives the person an exit without escalation. You do not have to be certain something is wrong to intervene. You do not have to be a hero. You just have to be the person who showed up. The festival community is only as safe as what each individual does in the moment they notice something.

**Tells:** THE DISTRACTION: walk up like you know her — "Oh my god, there you are! I have been looking everywhere for you!" / THE RESOURCE: find a Ranger or Harm Reduction worker, describe location and exactly what you saw / THE CHECK-IN: wait for a private moment and ask her directly — "Hey — are you okay?" / You do NOT have to confront the person causing concern — that is not required and not always safe

**Check:** S.A.F.E. —"S" CHECK: "What did I actually see — and which of the three moves fits this situation?"

**The dark version (Reading A Situation Quickly):** Signs a situation may need intervention: someone who seems significantly more impaired than their companions and is being directed or moved somewhere. Body language that is closed, small, or fearful in contrast to the other person's open or dominant posture. Someone who looks uncomfortable when you make eye contact with them and then looks away quickly. Someone being separated from their group by another person. A transaction that looks pressured or coerced. You do not need certainty to intervene with a distraction or to alert a Ranger. You just need a gut feeling. Trust it.

**The move:** Pick one of the three moves and do it. Doing something imperfectly is always better than doing nothing perfectly.

**Say:** Oh my god, there you are! I have been looking everywhere — come on, everyone is at the stage.

**The truth:** You do not need to be certain. You need to be the person who showed up.

### SEE SOMETHING — DO SOMETHING

**Section:** SEE SOMETHING

**Archetype:** Reading Impairment and Knowing When to Stay

**Clinical name:** Harm Reduction / Impairment Assessment

Being altered is a normal part of many festival experiences. Being so altered that you cannot take care of yourself, communicate clearly, or make safe decisions is a different situation — and the people around you are your safety net when you are in that state.

Your friend is having a bigger night than she intended. She is not in medical trouble but she is very out of it — she cannot navigate independently, she is making decisions that she would not make with clearer judgment, and she is attracting attention from some people who seem very interested in being helpful in ways that feel off to you. This is the moment where the buddy system is not a suggestion. It is the thing that determines how her night ends.

Severe impairment in a festival environment is a specific vulnerability state that predatory people specifically target. The person who cannot walk straight, cannot communicate clearly, and cannot make coherent decisions cannot consent to anything and cannot protect themselves from anyone. If your friend or someone near you is in this state — stay with them. Get them to a safe space. If they need medical attention get it without hesitation. Good Samaritan laws exist for exactly this situation.

**Tells:** Cannot navigate or find their way without help — beyond just being unfamiliar with the layout / Communication is significantly impaired — not just slow but unable to form coherent sentences / Making decisions that are completely out of character — especially about people or locations / Others are clustering around them in ways that feel more predatory than supportive

**Check:** S.A.F.E. — "E" CHECK: "Can this person take care of themselves right now — and if not, who is actually with them?"

**The dark version (When To Go To Medical Or Harm Reduction):** Go immediately: if they are unresponsive or semi-conscious, if their breathing is wrong, if their lips or fingertips are blue, if they had a seizure, if they have a high fever with no sweating. Go sooner rather than later: if they are significantly confused and getting more so, if they took something unknown and are reacting badly, if they are very distressed and not calming down, if you are worried and not sure why. The Harm Reduction tent is specifically for situations where you are not sure — they will help you assess. You will not get in trouble for bringing someone for care. You might regret not doing it.

**The move:** Stay with them. Get them to a safe space. Go to Harm Reduction or medical if you are unsure. Do not leave them alone.

**Say:** I am staying with you — we are going to find somewhere quiet. Come on.

**The truth:** The buddy who stays is the reason the story has a good ending. Be that person.

### KNOW THESE LOCATIONS BEFORE NIGHT ONE

**Section:** EMERGENCY

**Archetype:** Medical Tent, Harm Reduction, Rangers — Before You Need Them

The single most effective thing you can do for your safety this weekend — before any substance, before any adventure, before anything — is to physically locate the medical tent, the Harm Reduction tent, and the Ranger station. Do this before your first night. With your whole group. Together.

It is Thursday afternoon and you have just arrived. Your camp is set up. The festival has not really started yet. Everything feels calm and oriented. This is the moment to walk the grounds with your group and find the three locations. Not Saturday night when you need them. Right now. The ten minutes you spend orienting yourselves right now are the ten minutes that could determine how a difficult situation on Saturday night resolves.

The gap between knowing where help is and not knowing where help is is the difference between a situation that gets addressed quickly and one that escalates because nobody knows what to do or where to go. In a medical emergency at a festival, every minute matters. Pre-mapping your resources is the preparation that costs nothing and is worth everything.

**Tells:** Medical tent: equipped for physical emergencies, serious injury, overdose, and heat illness / Harm Reduction tent: equipped for substance-related distress, difficult experiences, non-judgmental support / Ranger station: event-specific safety response, less clinical, often better for social or interpersonal situations / Note the nearest water stations and shade structures to all three locations

**Check:** S.A.F.E. — "F" CHECK: "Have I physically located the medical tent, Harm Reduction tent, and Ranger station with my...

**The dark version (The Five-Minute Group Orientation):** Before your first night — as a group: (1) Walk to the medical tent together. Note the route and a visual landmark nearby. (2) Walk to the Harm Reduction tent. Note the route. (3) Walk to the Ranger station. Note the route. (4) Identify the nearest water station to your camp. (5) Choose a physical meeting spot for if you get separated — not a GPS location, a physical landmark. "The big wooden arch at the east entrance." (6) Set a daily check-in time. "We touch base at 2pm every day no matter what." This is ten minutes that changes the whole weekend.

**The move:** Do the five-minute group orientation before your first night. Every member of your group. Together.

**Say:** "Before we get into it tonight — let us all walk to the medical tent and Harm Reduction together so we know where they are."

**The truth:** Knowing where help is before you need it is the preparation. Everything else builds on that.

### IF SOMEONE GOES MISSING

**Section:** EMERGENCY

**Archetype:** What to Do and How Fast to Do It

Someone in your group is missing and you have not been able to reach them. This happens at festivals and it is almost always fine — and it is also sometimes not fine. The protocol for the first category and the second category is the same: act early, not late.

It is 4am and your campmate said she was going to the bathroom two hours ago and has not come back. Her phone is going to voicemail. You have checked the spot where you usually meet up. You have asked around the camp. Nobody has seen her. You are torn between assuming she is fine and having a good time somewhere and the feeling in your gut that something is wrong. The feeling in your gut is data.

There is no required waiting period before reporting a missing person at a festival. The idea that you must wait 24 hours is a myth and in a festival context is actively dangerous. Report as soon as you are concerned. Festival security, Rangers, and medical teams all have protocols for missing persons. The earlier you report, the better the resources available, and the better the outcomes. Your concern is sufficient reason to make a report.

**Tells:** Report to festival security or Rangers as soon as you are concerned — there is no minimum time to wait / Have her photo available on your phone before you go to report — full face, what she is wearing today / Know what she was last wearing — write it down when you last saw her, not when she is missing / Check the medical tent and Harm Reduction tent before assuming the worst — she may already be safe there

**Check:** S.A.F.E. — "E" CHECK: "Have I already checked medical and Harm Reduction tents — and do I have her photo on my...

**The dark version (Missing Person Information To Have Ready):** When you report: (1) Full name and age. (2) Physical description including height, approximate weight, hair color. (3) What she was wearing the last time you saw her — be specific including shoes. (4) Last known location and time. (5) Phone number and whether it is going to voicemail. (6) Her relationship to alcohol and substances this weekend and any medical conditions. (7) A clear recent photo from your phone. Write some of this down when you last see members of your group each day — not when they are missing and you are panicking.

**The move:** Check medical and Harm Reduction tents first. If not found — report to Rangers immediately with photo and description.

**Say:** I need to report a missing person — she has been gone for two hours and is not answering her phone.

**The truth:** There is no minimum wait time to report a missing person at a festival. Report when you are concerned. Not after.

### DIFFICULT TRIPS AND PSYCHOLOGICAL CRISES

**Section:** MENTAL HEALTH

**Archetype:** What They Look Like and What Actually Helps

**Clinical name:** Adverse Psychedelic Experience / Crisis Support

Difficult psychological experiences at festivals — sometimes called bad trips, sometimes genuine mental health crises — are common, often preventable, and very treatable when handled correctly. The wrong response can make them significantly worse.

Your friend took something three hours ago and for the first two hours he was having the time of his life. Now something has shifted. He is very frightened — he cannot articulate why exactly but everything feels threatening. He is trying to leave the area but you are not sure where he wants to go or if he should be alone right now. He is not in physical danger but he is clearly in real psychological distress and getting more so.

Difficult psychedelic or substance-induced experiences are almost always temporary and almost always respond well to calm, quiet, grounded support. The worst things you can do: try to talk them out of their experience, argue with them about what is real, leave them alone, or take them to a loud and stimulating environment. The Zendo Project specializes specifically in difficult psychedelic experience support and has trained staff at many major festivals. Get them there if available.

**Tells:** Intense fear or panic without a clear external cause / Disorientation or confusion about what is real vs not real / Wanting to escape urgently without a clear destination or reason / Physical symptoms accompanying the psychological distress: rapid breathing, shaking, sweating

**Check:** S.A.F.E. — "F" CHECK: "Do I know where the Zendo or Harm Reduction tent is — and can I get us both there calmly...

**The dark version (What Actually Helps In A Difficult Experience):** What helps: move to a quieter, lower-stimulation space. Sit with them on the ground. Speak calmly and slowly. Remind them where they are and that they are physically safe. "You took something, it will pass, I am right here, you are safe." Do not try to fix or solve the experience — just be a calm presence in it. What does not help: arguing about what is real, leaving them alone, taking them to a loud or crowded space, giving them more substances, or panicking yourself. Zendo Project (zendoproject.org): specifically trained for this. Get them there if you can.

**The move:** Get to a quiet space. Sit down together. Stay calm. Get to Harm Reduction or Zendo if it is not resolving.

**Say:** I am right here. You are safe. You took something and it will pass. I am not going anywhere.

**The truth:** A calm presence is the most powerful intervention in a difficult experience. You have that.

### SIGNS OF A GENUINE MENTAL HEALTH CRISIS

**Section:** MENTAL HEALTH

**Archetype:** When It Is More Than a Difficult Trip

**Clinical name:** Mental Health Crisis / Psychiatric Emergency

Festival environments can precipitate genuine mental health crises in people who may have underlying vulnerabilities — the combination of sleep deprivation, substances, emotional intensity, and social pressure can tip someone into a state that requires more than peer support.

Your campmate has been quiet and withdrawn since Saturday morning. He came out once, said he was fine, went back in. It is Sunday and you have knocked twice and he said he was okay but something in his voice did not match the word. He has not eaten anything you know of. He made a comment last night about not being sure why he came. You are sitting with the question of whether to push harder to check on him. Push harder.

Mental health crises at festivals are underreported and underidentified because the expectation is that everyone is having a good time and should be having a good time. The signs can be subtle and easy to attribute to tiredness or a difficult trip. If your gut says something is wrong with someone in your group — check on them again. Ask directly. "Are you actually okay — because I am asking for real." The question asked with genuine care and real eye contact is a different question than the passing "you okay?"

**Tells:** Withdrawal from the festival for an extended period when they came to engage / Saying they are fine in a way that does not match their voice, energy, or behavior / Comments that sound like they feel hopeless, purposeless, or like they should not have come / Not eating, not sleeping, not coming out — for longer than exhaustion alone explains

**Check:** S.A.F.E. — "S" CHECK: "What is my gut actually telling me about this person — and am I acting on it?"

**The dark version (Crisis Resources At Festivals):** Zendo Project (zendoproject.org): difficult psychedelic experiences and mental health crisis — present at many major festivals. Festival medical tents: handle mental health crises as well as physical ones. 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline: call or text 988, 24/7, trained crisis counselors. Crisis Text Line: text HOME to 741741 — useful when a voice call feels too hard. If someone says anything that sounds like they are thinking about hurting themselves: take it seriously, stay with them, get help. "I am just going to make sure you are okay — come on, let us go find somewhere quiet together."

**The move:** Knock again. Ask directly. Stay with them. Get help if needed. Do not normalize what your gut is flagging.

**Say:** I am asking for real — are you actually okay? Because I am not going anywhere until I know.

**The truth:** Checking on your people is not being dramatic. It is being a real friend.

### THE MORNING AFTER

**Section:** COMMUNITY CARE

**Archetype:** Processing Difficult Experiences Without Shame

**Clinical name:** Post-Incident Support / Trauma-Informed Care

Something happened this weekend. Or you watched something happen. Or you supported someone through something. Or you are sitting with something you cannot fully name yet. This page is for Sunday — the quiet one when everything gets real.

It is Sunday afternoon and the festival is winding down and you are sitting with something heavy. You are not sure if you are being dramatic. You are not sure who to tell. You are exhausted and overwhelmed and every feeling is louder than normal. Here is what is true: you do not have to have it figured out to deserve support. You do not need a perfect story. You do not need certainty about what happened or what to call it. You just need to reach out.

Post-incident processing is hard enough without the additional weight of deciding whether your experience is bad enough to matter. It is. Whatever level of severity it sits at — it matters. You do not have to be okay by the time you load the car. You do not have to have it figured out before you talk to someone. Give yourself the first 48-72 hours for basic recovery — sleep, food, low stimulation — before trying to process fully. Then talk to someone you trust.

**Tells:** You do not need a perfect memory or perfect certainty to deserve support — reach out anyway / Harm Reduction tents are confidential and are not required to report to law enforcement / RAINN hotline (1-800-656-4673) is 24/7 and supports all genders and all types of experiences / Crisis Text Line: text HOME to 741741 if talking feels too hard right now

**Check:** S.A.F.E. — "E" CHECK: "What does my body actually need right now — not what does the situation require me to...

**The dark version (The Week After The Festival):** Post-festival blues are real — the crash from social intensity, sleep debt, and substances can feel significant and last several days. What helps: sleep, real food, gentle movement, low stimulation, reconnecting with people you trust. What makes it harder: immediately jumping back into intensity, isolating, making major decisions in the first week. If you are carrying something heavy from the weekend: give yourself 3-5 days before trying to process it fully. If the weight does not lift after a week or two — reach out to a therapist. Psychology Today (psychologytoday.com) has a therapist finder by specialty including trauma.

**The move:** Eat. Sleep. Tell one trusted person. Reach out to resources if you need more. You deserve support.

**Say:** Something happened this weekend I am still processing. I do not need you to fix it — I just need you to hear it.

**The truth:** You do not have to be okay yet. Support exists. You deserve it.

### THE BUDDY SYSTEM

**Section:** COMMUNITY CARE

**Archetype:** How to Actually Run One That Works

**Clinical name:** Peer Safety / Community Protection Protocol

The buddy system is not childish. It is the single most effective community safety tool at a festival and the people who use it consistently have better and safer experiences than the people who do not. Here is how to actually run one.

Friday morning before the festival opens your group sits down for ten minutes. Everyone says who their buddy is for the weekend. You set a check-in time — 2pm every day, you physically find each other or send a specific text. You choose a code word that means "come find me right now, no questions." You agree on a meeting spot for if you get separated. You agree that if anyone does not check in on time someone comes looking. Ten minutes. That is the whole investment.

The buddy system works because it creates accountability before you need it — not after something has already gone wrong. The check-in time means that if something is wrong you know within hours, not days. The code word means you can signal for help in any situation without making a scene. The meeting spot means you can reconnect without phone signal. These are not elaborate safety measures. They are ten minutes of planning that changes the entire risk profile of your weekend.

**Tells:** Name your buddy before gates open — not organically during the festival / Set a specific daily check-in time with a specific response expected, not just "stay in touch" / Choose a code word that means "come find me immediately" — something you would not say normally / Agree on a physical meeting spot that does not require phone signal to find

**Check:** S.A.F.E. — "E" CHECK: "Does everyone in my group have a buddy and do we all have the code word and the...

**The dark version (The Full Buddy System Checklist):** Before gates open: (1) Every person has a named buddy. (2) Daily check-in time agreed — specific time, specific expected response. (3) Code word chosen — something normal-sounding that means "come get me now." (4) Physical meeting spot chosen — a Specific landmark, not a GPS point. (5) One person not at the festival has the names and phone numbers of everyone in the group. (6) Everyone writes two emergency numbers in pen on their arm — their buddy and one person at home. (7) Offline festival map downloaded on at least two phones. Ten minutes. Do it before the music starts.

**The move:** Do the buddy system setup with your whole group before gates open. All of it. Ten minutes.

**Say:** Before we go in — ten minutes to sort the buddy system. Who is with who? What is the code word? What is the meeting spot?

**The truth:** The ten minutes you spend on this before the festival is the preparation that matters most.

### THE BATHROOM BUDDY AND NIGHT WALKS

**Section:** COMMUNITY CARE

**Archetype:** Simple Habits That Significantly Reduce Risk

**Clinical name:** Personal Safety / Environmental Risk Reduction

Some of the simplest safety habits in festival culture are also the most consistently overlooked — especially as the weekend progresses and everyone gets more comfortable and more tired. These are the ones worth making non-negotiable.

It is 3am Saturday. You need the bathroom and your campmate is finally asleep and your buddy is at a set you did not want to see. The walk to the bathroom is maybe four minutes through a part of the festival that is less busy at this hour. You almost go alone. The almost is the moment. The almost is where the simple habit matters.

Most adverse incidents at festivals happen in low-visibility, low-traffic situations in the later hours of the night — on walks between stages, in bathroom queues, in the paths between camping areas. The simple habit of not being alone in those moments is one of the most effective risk reductions available. It does not require elaborate systems. It just requires the group norm that you do not let people walk alone after dark.

**Tells:** Bathroom buddy rule: someone walks with you to the bathroom after dark, every time, no exceptions / Walking each other back to tents after late sets — do not let altered friends navigate alone in the dark / Phone charging as safety infrastructure — share chargers freely, a dead phone is a vulnerability / The quiet check-in: "you good?" before splitting up is two seconds and it matters

**Check:** S.A.F.E. — "E" CHECK: "Is anyone in our group alone right now in a situation where they should not be?"

**The dark version (Night Navigation Safety Basics):** After dark at festivals: bring a headlamp or phone torch — being able to see and be seen is basic safety. Stay on lit paths between stages when possible. If you are going somewhere unfamiliar or less trafficked, tell your buddy where you are going and when you expect to be back. If you feel uncomfortable on a walk — trust that. Change direction, find a busier path, head toward lights and sound. Ranger patrols concentrate on darker and less trafficked areas — if you see a Ranger and feel unsafe, walk with them or near them. You do not have to explain why.

**The move:** Bathroom buddy rule. Walk each other back. Share your charger. Two seconds to check in before splitting up.

**Say:** I am going to the bathroom — come with me? I will walk you back after.

**The truth:** The simplest habits are the ones that matter most. Make them non-negotiable in your group.

### COMMUNITY BEYOND YOUR CAMP

**Section:** COMMUNITY CARE

**Archetype:** Looking Out for Strangers as Well as Your People

**Clinical name:** Community Safety / Prosocial Behavior

The festival community is only as safe as what each person does when they see something that does not involve their own crew. This page is about extending your care beyond your group — because the stranger you help might be someone else's buddy who needs exactly what you have to offer.

You are at the water station and a woman comes up alone and asks if you can spare a phone charger for ten minutes. She says she got separated from her group and cannot reach anyone. She seems okay but uncertain. You have a portable charger. The ten minutes it takes to help her reconnect with her group might be the ten minutes that means her story has a good ending. This is what community care actually looks like — not grand gestures, small ones.

Community safety at festivals is not the responsibility of staff and Rangers alone — it is a distributed responsibility across every person there. The small acts of community care — sharing a charger, asking if someone is okay, walking someone to the Harm Reduction tent, using the distraction approach for a stranger in a difficult situation — these are what make the difference between a festival where harm happens and goes unaddressed and one where the community catches things before they escalate.

**Tells:** Share your phone charger freely — a charged phone is safety infrastructure for everyone / If someone seems lost or uncertain — ask if they need help finding something / If you see someone alone and very impaired — check in, do not assume someone else will / If you are leaving a set and see someone alone at the edge who looks uncomfortable — the distraction approach works on strangers too

**Check:** S.A.F.E. — "E" CHECK: "Am I extending care beyond my own group — or only looking out for the people I know?"

**The dark version (What Festie Community Care Actually Looks Like):** Real community care in practice: share your charger without being asked. Ask the person sitting alone if they are waiting for someone or need help. Walk someone to a resource if they seem unsure where to go. Give your extra water to someone who clearly needs it. Use the distraction approach for a stranger in a situation that looks off. Tell a Ranger what you saw. These are not heroic acts. They are the distributed infrastructure of a safe festival. When everyone does the small things the big things happen less often.

**The move:** Extend your care beyond your camp. The small acts of community care are the infrastructure of a safe festival.

**Say:** "Hey — do you need help finding something? I have a charger if your phone is dead."

**The truth:** The festival is only as safe as what each person does when they see something. That includes you.
