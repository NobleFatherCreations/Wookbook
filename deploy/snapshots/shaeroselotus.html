Read straight from your official Astrodienst data sheet — every planet, angle, node and Chiron by exact degree, sign, house and dignity; then a clear section on what each placement means, the real aspects, the chart's architecture, the five-fold signature, and your live timing. Built to be handed to an astrologer.

A natal chart is not a metaphor — it is a photograph. It records the actual positions of the planets along the ecliptic (the Sun's apparent path, divided into twelve 30° signs) at 18:01 UT on 16 December 1985, as seen from Wimbledon. Nothing in it is interpreted yet; the interpretation is a second, separate act laid on top of measured astronomy.

Two parts of the photograph depend on the minute and the place, and they are the parts that make a chart personal. The Ascendant is whichever degree of the zodiac was climbing over the eastern horizon at that instant — it advances roughly one degree every four minutes, so an hour's error in a birth time hands you a different rising sign and reshuffles every house. The Moon moves about half a degree per hour, fast enough that a birth date without a time leaves it uncertain. Everything else barely moves in a day. So when this map leans on your Cancer rising, your 8th-house Moon and your Placidus cusps, it is leaning on the precision of that 18:01 — treat the time as part of the data.

Placidus, the house system used here, divides the sky by time rather than by equal slices of space: it asks how long each degree takes to rise from the horizon to the midheaven. At London's high latitude that stretching gets extreme — some signs take hours to rise, others race — which is exactly what seals Libra and Aries whole inside houses 4 and 10 (the interceptions). Different house systems slice the same sky differently and can disagree at high latitudes, which is why a serious chart always names its system. Yours does.
